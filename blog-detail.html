The Complete Guide to French Polishing

12 July 2025 8 min read Thomas Wren

French polish gives antique furniture its characteristic deep, glassy sheen — a finish that no modern lacquer can replicate. Understanding the process helps you care for, identify, and appreciate the pieces that carry it.

A Brief History

French polishing emerged in France during the early 19th century and spread rapidly through Europe as the dominant cabinet finishing technique. By the Victorian era, virtually every quality piece of English furniture left a workshop with a French-polished surface.

The finish is created by applying many thin layers of shellac — a natural resin secreted by the lac bug — dissolved in denatured alcohol, using a rubber (a pad of fabric wrapped around a cotton core). The resulting finish is extraordinarily deep and transparent.

"A good French polish is not a coating — it is a conversation between the wood and the finisher, built over many sessions, each layer revealing more of what lies beneath." — Thomas Wren, Master Cabinetmaker

How to Identify French Polish

French-polished furniture has several telltale characteristics:

  • An extremely deep, glass-like reflective surface
  • Visible wood grain beneath the finish (never obscured)
  • Sensitivity to heat and water — white rings form easily
  • A slightly warm, amber tonality compared to modern finishes
  • Surface flex — French polish moves slightly with the wood

Caring for French Polished Furniture

French polish requires more care than modern lacquered surfaces, but properly maintained it can last centuries.

Professional Tip: The Water Test

If water left on the surface for 30 seconds leaves a white mark, the finish is shellac-based. This marks it as French polish or a shellac-based finish requiring specialist care.

Daily Care

Use a soft, lint-free cotton cloth slightly dampened with distilled water. Never use silicone-based polishes — they seal the surface and make future restoration impossible. A light application of beeswax furniture polish two to three times per year is ideal.

Avoiding Damage

  • Always use coasters and heat-proof mats
  • Keep out of direct sunlight — UV causes yellowing and cracking
  • Maintain humidity between 45–55% — extreme dryness causes cracking
  • Never use chemical cleaners, spray polishes, or water in quantity

When to Call a Professional

French polish can be repaired by professionals to a nearly invisible standard. If you notice white rings, surface cloudiness, cracking, or areas where the finish has completely worn through, it's time to consult a specialist.

Attempting DIY repair of French polish almost always causes more damage — the solvents in most hardware store products are incompatible with shellac and will dissolve the surrounding finish unevenly.
